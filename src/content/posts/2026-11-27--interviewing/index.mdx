Nowadays, I make my living as a leadership advisor and interim leader. So I don't interview for full-time jobs very often. But I came across one that seemed unusually interesting, and I applied for it.

<Image src={interviewsImage} alt="a pretty landscape" width={800} />

My interview went hilariously bad. I was probably one of the most qualified people in the world for this position. But friends, *I wouldn’t have hired me* based on that interview.

I answered questions poorly. I didn’t come up with convincing responses to obvious questions. I skipped clarifying questions. And when I looked back on how I answered things, I didn’t really make the case around the things I was being interviewed for.

On reflection, I remembered that interviewing was a skill that never came naturally to me. Earlier in my career, I spent a lot of time getting better at it. But when I had prepared for this interview, I hadn't tapped into all of those things I had learned in the past.

So I'd like to write down, for your benefit and for mine, things I've learned about how to interview well. I'll follow up in later posts with other things that I've learned about writing resumes, and some advice for early career folks. But let’s start with interviewing.

I've done a ton of interviewing in my career. I've hired or helped to hire 10 heads of engineering. I've interviewed hundreds of engineers. I have a lot of perspective from both sides.

## Why interviews go wrong

The first thing is that interviews are an unnatural situation that doesn't really reflect what the job is going to be like. You often have no idea what the real criteria are for the role.

Interviews are stressful. You might have anxiety going into an interview. As a result, you may not be at your best.

Some people can naturally shine without preparation. But I find for me, preparation makes everything work. The important thing is *how* I prepare.

My memory is a little weird. I have a lot of difficulty coming up with examples from very broad questions. The most common way people are interviewed nowadays is behavioral interviews. “Tell me about a time when…” I'm just terrible at that kind of recall, because it seems too broad and I have difficulty conjuring examples.

During the interview that I flubbed, this meant that I failed to come up with some examples that would have made a better case to the hiring manager. Does that sound familiar to you?

## Be present, ask clarifying questions, have flexible length responses

The thing that surprised me in the bombed interview was how differently I acted from my usual advisory work. At the root of it, I suspect it was because I wasn't thinking about how I wanted to show up.

What I’m doing for myself is to come up with a reminder of how to approach the interview.

My reminder is:
1. **Be in the moment**.
2. **Listen carefully**, don’t just think about how I want to respond.
3. **Ask clarifying questions**.
4. **Answer concisely but ask** if they want more detail.

You may have a different version of reminders for your strengths and weaknesses. Let me explain why I chose these for myself.

Often there is a question behind the question being asked. When I think back to my bombed interview, there were a couple of major questions that the hiring manager was trying to answer for themselves. They didn't necessarily ask those questions in the best way. By being more present, I could have clarified and focused my answers in a way that really hammered home the areas that they were wanting to address.

It's a good practice to clarify intent or even ask, are you trying to understand ABC or DEF? Then aim to answer in a few minutes, and then check in to see if they want more detail and if you answered what they wanted to know.

## Prepare stories ahead of time

One thing I've noticed interviewing others is that **the people that stand out in my memory are the people that tell stories**. Humans naturally respond well to narrative.

So one of the most important skills I've had to learn is storytelling. And what has worked for me is to prepare stories ahead of time. A lot of stories.

The way I do that is to prepare by **making a list of every project I've ever been a part of**, at every company. If you're later in your career, it probably doesn't have to be exhaustive. But the idea is to prepare a bunch of stories that you can tell. For each project, **make a list of a couple of points that you can make** with that project.

Put the stories on an index card or sticky. Here’s an example:

**Situation**: Took over as director of the XYZ team when it had low morale and wasn’t shipping. High pressure because there was a new realization that it was critical to the future of the company.

**Task**: Diagnose the team and delivery issues, make it better, fast.

**Action**: Intensively interview the team and investigate how work was being done, look at goals and plans, work with manager to get everything on track quickly.

**Result**: we went from problem team to a model team in six months. Shipped a new demo of new product direction within a month. Team shipped great work and had higher morale.

**What points I can make**:
- Importance of digging in to understand details and not trusting the convenient narrative
- Importance of alignment and the difference a single person can make on a team.
- Kickoffs and their importance, and how a vacation plan forced me to do the best kickoff of my life.

This uses the [STAR](https://capd.mit.edu/resources/the-star-method-for-behavioral-interviews/) format.

**Practice telling these stories** until it’s smooth, and you can use them to tell any of the points.

When you’re in an interview, you’ll get asked a lot of questions. Your job then is to **choose from your menu of stories**, and pick the story that best makes the point you want to make, but as an anecdote.

Why does this work? A story makes the point more effectively than anything else. It provides some solid proof beyond just you asserting it. And, it’s memorable, so the interviewer will walk away thinking, “wow, that candidate that told the story about handling the teams that fell apart, that was great”.

## Prepare for the common questions

Because behavioral interviewing is so common, it's probably a good idea to think about the **most common behavioral questions you'll be asked**. What are the things that someone would ask about during this interview?
- They’re definitely going to ask about something that was hard in the last year.
- They’re going to ask about how you handled disagreements and working with others.
- They’ll ask something very specific to the role, that is hard. Managers are asked about letting someone go. Product leaders are asked about putting together a product strategy.

These type of questions are so common that it is hurting yourself if you're not preparing for them. Make sure you have index cards for them.

## After the interview

**You can follow up after an interview**. One thing I've done in the past when I forget to make a point is to follow up with an email afterwards that describes a better response to their question. Often there is a natural followup you can do that shows that you go the extra mile and do good work.

**Practice and introspect**. A final thing that I will share about interviewing is that it's a skill and it's one you should practice. After each interview, try to critique what went well and what didn't. If you're using an AI note-taker, have it critique how you responded. Try to set up some guardrails for you so that each interview is better than the last. If there is a job you really are interested in, have a friend do a practice interview for you.

## What I'll do differently next time

I bombed the interview this time. Here are a few things I’ll do better next time:
1. I’ll give myself a few reminders to help me be more present, to clarify intent and adapt the amount of detail.
2. I’ll prepare my index cards with stories ahead of time, and have a huge menu of them I’ve practiced and rehearsed.

If you have anything to add to this list, let me know. I might update the article with it!
