In the [last post](/interviewing/), I discussed things I've learned about interviewing. In this post, I'm going to talk about how to improve your resume. This is something I've had a stronger track record with. I've been helping people with their resumes for decades now. I've also been on the receiving end of this, reviewing thousands of resumes over the years.

## Your resume has several audiences

Nowadays, your resume is serving three or four different audiences. It will be reviewed by
1. a recruiter
2. (possibly) an AI
3. the interviewers
4. and finally the hiring manager

**Recruiters and AIs** are going to survey your resume in a similar way. They're going to look at it to see if you meet the criteria of the role, and they'll try to make some sort of assessment of how good of a fit you are for it. Often they’ll be using heuristics or keywords to eliminate people.

Most people optimize their resumes for this audience. Thus, you'll see engineers list a bunch of languages and frameworks. And you’ll have a list of things you’re responsible for or skills you have.

**Interviewers and the hiring manager** look for the same things. But they additionally are looking for someone that excites them. Most resumes are pretty boring. People use the same boring language and formats in their resumes. The resumes read like *blah blah blah blah blah blah blah*. I'm not suggesting that your primary goal is to catch attention, but it’s pretty easy to stand out.

## Show the diff

One of the easiest things to do to make your resume more impactful is to turn it **from a list of responsibilities to a list of results and things you made happen**. Say what it was like before and after you did your work. Talk about the impact it had on the customer, on the company, or on your team.

Yes, it’s important for people to know what you were responsible for, but it’s way more impressive if you show how you affected that area of responsibility:

“Led two engineering teams” is less interesting than, “Led two engineering teams that were considered hot spots and within a year was asked to give a presentation on why they were so effective”, or “I started out managing two teams with low morale and high turnover, and within six months, we had the second best eNPS score in engineering”.

So: “Show the diff”.

## Let other people brag for you

Another thing you can do that I don't see anywhere is to **use other people's words to brag**. I use quotes from former bosses, from peers, from direct reports, from customers, from even candidates that I've interviewed to paint a picture of how I make a difference at work.

For example:
- “The CTO told me I was the best communicator in the entire company”.
- “A fellow employee told me that he thinks I contributed more to the company’s success in the last three years I worked there than anyone else.”

I make these anonymous unless I’ve specifically asked the person for a recommendation.

How do you remember the quotes? When I hear something that makes me glow from a coworker or boss or etc., I'll write it down into my own personal [brag list](https://www.nadyadukeboone.com/keep-an-achievements-and-mistakes-list/).

## Tell a mini-story

If you can **tell a mini-story** as you describe the impact you have on a company, that can also give people an idea of what type of employee you are and how you show up at work.

In one version of my resume, I talked about how I built an intranet for the company, and I talked about the impact it had. But then I mentioned that I realized that just creating the intranet wasn't enough and that I really needed to work with people to internalize it in their workflows, and I went about doing that.

This shows I am someone that doesn’t just get something done, but I learn and adapt, and try to drive the work through to business results. It sounds way better than saying I built an intranet and trained people on it.

When telling stories like this, the main challenge is to keep it concise.

## Write with approachable language

I try to **use plain, first person language** in my resume. I want it to sound like I'm in front of the person talking to them. I don’t want it to feel like they’re reading a generic list of skills on a generic person they’ve never met.

A lot of resumes are so formal that they're hard to read. Formality makes sense in some contexts, and being informal may disqualify you in those contexts. But when you think there is flexibility, consider whether a less formal approach will be better.

## Format your resume nicely

Most resumes look the same. They rely on the same templates and formatting. When I come across a resume with nice formatting and typography, and nice colors, it stands out. This is especially relevant for roles where design is a factor, but that little bit of extra attention can help anywhere.
