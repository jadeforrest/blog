I occasionally talk with people that are in college and want to make sure they can find a job after they graduate. It’s become significantly harder in the software industry to land a job after graduation than it used to be.

So I’ve been collecting suggestions to share with people that are early-career in competitive fields. If you think it’s useful, please share it with early career folks in your network. And if you see advice that is missing, let me know, so I can update this post.

## Start building experience early

My first recommendation is to start the clock early. One failure mode I see in people that are looking for work in a field is that they focus all of their attention on their school or on their job search. I can understand why they do that. It makes a lot of sense to focus on what is immediately ahead of you.

But one of the most immediate ways that people evaluate you is by how much experience you have.

If your job experience when you graduate looks like you have no experience at all and you contrast that against somebody that has three years of experience, you can see how the person with more experience is going to have the edge. Every year of experience will make it easier for you to get a role, so your job is to make every year another impressive year of experience, even if you don’t have the job yet!

There are a lot of ways that you can start the clock:
- You can do side projects.
- You can volunteer for an organization.
- You can do internships or co-ops.
- If you're especially ambitious, you can start a company. Perhaps in a Lean Startup sort of way. A new grad with a failed startup they ran for 2 years looks pretty interesting.

Yes, there is a little bit of privilege built into this recommendation. It's a lot like extracurricular activities in high school helping to get you into college, but I would argue it's maybe even more important.

## Do the work as if you’re being paid

Let's say that you're interested in journalism. Yes, if you can, you should get a job at your school paper. But even if you're not able to do that, you should pretend that you do have a job at the local paper. You could write a blog with your “job”. You could have a podcast with your “job”.

Then several years later, when you're looking for work, you can point to your regular articles or podcasts as evidence that you have already been practicing.

If you're interested in software engineering, write a lot of software.

## Lead with what you already know

Another thing to keep in mind is that often if you are inexperienced in one area, you may be very experienced in another. For example, I've seen people with a lot of industry experience sometimes land roles as product managers even though they have no product management experience.

If you’re good at public speaking, or good at facilitating conversations, remember that those are things you will provide immediate value at even if you’re new to other parts of your role.

If you have an area of expertise, you can sometimes use that as a lever into a different role, or as a way to differentiate yourself from others.

## Build your network

A lot of roles are found through people you know. You might even want to relocate to be at the hub of where your type of work is most in demand.

So be deliberate about this. How can you increase the number of people connected to this type of work?

For example, if you’re interested in a job in politics, are you volunteering in political campaigns? Are there events or meetings you should make a habit of attending? Do you know others doing similar work? Can you bring people together with common interests?
